5. Find the satellite in the sky

The dashboard lists passes hours before they happen. How does a small board that cannot see the sky know where a satellite will be?

It works it out. Soon after it starts, and every six hours after that, the board downloads a short description of each satellite’s orbit. From that description and the date and time, it calculates where the satellite is, where the dish is, and the direction from one to the other. Every step is geometry you can watch happen. This chapter follows one weather satellite, METOP-B, through those steps. By the end you will be able to read the function that does the work and recognise each line.

Two lines of text

Here is everything the board is told about METOP-B’s orbit. It is one record from the file ezal’s own tests use, and it describes the orbit as it was at 07:42:59 UTC on 7 October 2026. A record in this format is called a two-line element set, or TLE.

Most of the characters are bookkeeping: a catalogue number, a launch code, a count of laps flown. Six numbers describe the orbit itself, and four of them shape the picture. Try each slider.

  • Tilt is how steeply the ring leans away from the equator. At 0° the satellite would circle above the equator for ever. At 90° it would pass over both poles. METOP-B leans a little beyond that, so as the Earth turns inside the ring, the satellite sees nearly all of it.
  • Facing says which way round the tilted ring sits. It is an angle around the equator, from a direction that stays fixed among the stars to the place where the satellite crosses the equator heading north.
  • Where is how far round the ring the satellite had got at the record’s date, counted from the mark labelled 0°.
  • Laps a day sets the size of the ring. Gravity ties the two together: the closer a satellite is, the faster it must go round. Slide it and watch the ring shrink and grow.

The other two numbers hardly change this picture. 0002825 means 0.0002825 and says how far the ring is from a perfect circle: almost not at all. 45.3235 says where on the ring its lowest point is, and that is where the 0° mark sits.

Watch the last digit of the second line as you drag. It is a check digit: add up every digit in the line, count each minus sign as 1, and keep the last digit of the total. If a character gets damaged on the way, the sum usually stops matching.

That matters, because these lines arrive over the network from a public catalogue, and the board does not trust them. Before anything else reads a line, well_formed requires exactly 69 characters, the right kind of character in every column, and a check digit that matches. Time passes

The record says where the satellite was at one moment. To find it at any other, ezal hands the record to a library called sgp4, named after the standard method for these records, along with one number: how many minutes have passed since the record’s date. Back come three numbers.

The three numbers are distances in kilometres from the centre of the Earth, measured along three axes that cross there at right angles. z runs up through the north pole. x and y lie in the plane of the equator, and x is the fixed direction from the first figure. These axes do not turn with the Earth: move the minutes slider and the land slides past them. The code calls this set of axes TEME.

Now step through the days. The dashed ring is where the orbit was at the record’s date. A satellite circling a perfectly round planet would keep to that ring for ever. The real Earth bulges at the equator, and the bulge’s extra pull swings the ring steadily round: about one degree a day for this orbit. (That rate is chosen. One degree a day keeps pace with the Earth’s year-long trip round the Sun, so the satellite comes over at the same time of day all year.)

There is also a trace of air, even 820 km up, and it slowly drags the satellite down. The record carries a number for that too. For METOP-B it adds up to about two kilometres in a week. The library allows for the bulge, the drag and several smaller effects. Nobody can predict the air perfectly, though, so a record goes stale, and ezal will not forecast more than seven days past a record’s date.

The figures on this page do not run the library. They move the satellite round a plain ellipse and add only the steady swing from the bulge. For the pass we are about to follow, that simpler model points within half a degree of what ezal calculates, and finds the same rise and set times to within two seconds. Everything after this step, from the turning Earth to the final angles, is the same arithmetic as ezal’s and gives the same answers.

The Earth turns underneath

The satellite’s ring is fixed to the stars. The dish is bolted to a planet that turns. Before one can be compared with the other, both must be measured along the same axes.

Hold the stars still and press Play. The Earth spins, and the satellite keeps to its ring. Now hold the Earth still. Nothing in the scene has changed, only where we watch it from, yet the satellite no longer retraces its path: each lap crosses the equator further west, because the ground has moved on underneath.

One angle separates the two views: how far the Greenwich meridian (the thick line down the globe) has turned past the fixed x axis. The code calls it the sidereal angle, and the figure marks it θ. The slider starts at the moment it was zero.

Drag the slider to its far end, 24 hours later, and read θ. It has not gone round once but slightly more: 361°. The Earth turns once against the stars in 23 hours 56 minutes. A day is longer because it runs from noon to noon, and by the next noon the Earth has moved along its path round the Sun and has to turn a little extra to face it again. So θ cannot be read off a clock. It needs the date as well.

To move a position from the axes fixed to the stars to the axes riding on the Earth, turn it back by θ about the z axis. Compare the two rows of numbers under the figure: x and y differ, and z never does.

Where we are standing

From here on we ride with the Earth. The dish’s position is given the way a map gives it: latitude 51.44266° north, longitude 2.84053° west, and a height. For the next step it has to become x, y and z as well, along the axes that turn with the Earth: x out through the equator on the Greenwich meridian, y out through the equator a quarter turn east, z through the north pole.

If the Earth were a ball, this would take one radius and some trigonometry. But the Earth is slightly flattened: its centre is 21 km nearer to the poles than to the equator. You cannot see that at true scale, so drag the second slider to exaggerate it.

Look at up. It stays square to the ground, the way a mast stands. On a flattened Earth that line no longer points away from the centre (the dashed grey line does that). Followed downward, it misses the centre and meets the Earth’s axis a little beyond it.

The code follows that dotted teal line. It works out the line’s length from the dish down to the axis and calls it n. How far the line reaches sideways gives x and y. How far it reaches vertically, less the stretch beyond the centre, gives z.

At true scale the lean is small, 0.19° at Clevedon, but it is not nothing. Treat the Earth as a ball and the dish’s position comes out 22 km wrong, which points the dish more than a degree off when this satellite passes. The function runs once. The dish does not move, so its position and the sines and cosines of its latitude and longitude are kept for every calculation that follows.

The arrow from the dish to the satellite

With both positions measured along the same axes, the direction from one to the other is a subtraction.

These are the first lines of the function this chapter has been heading for. The two steps you have just watched, turning back by θ and subtracting the dish, happen together.

East, north and up

The arrow is right, but not yet useful. Its three numbers are measured along axes through Greenwich and the pole, and the rotator knows nothing of those. As chapter 2 described, it turns about a vertical mast (azimuth) and tilts up from level (elevation). So the last step measures the same arrow along three directions the dish does know: east, north and up.

The sheet is flat and the Earth is not, so the ground curves away beneath it. The satellite is above the horizon exactly while it is above this sheet. Play the pass and watch it come up through the sheet in the south-east and go back down through it in the north.

The arrow has not changed. The figure only breaks it into three new legs: so far east, so far north, so far up. The pale blue path lays the first two on the sheet, and it ends directly beneath the satellite, at the end of the arrow’s shadow.

The two angles the rotator wants come straight from the legs. Azimuth is the compass bearing of the shadow: start facing north and turn clockwise until you face along it. Elevation is how far the arrow tilts up from its shadow.

How do you measure an arrow along a new direction? Multiply each of the arrow’s three numbers by the matching number of the direction, and add up. East at Clevedon, written along the Earth’s axes, is (0.0496, 0.9988, 0). At 19:46:00 the arrow is (858, 840, 322). So the arrow reaches 0.0496 × 858 + 0.9988 × 840 + 0 × 322 = 881 km east. Both figures open at 19:46:00, so you can check that against them.

That is all of look_at: turn, subtract, measure. Its answer is the straight line from the dish to the satellite. It takes no account of hills and buildings on the skyline or of the slight bending of radio waves in the air. And exactly overhead the shadow has no length, so the bearing means nothing there.

Twelve hours of asking

That is one direction for one moment. The dashboard wants to know when the satellite will next be in view, so ezal asks the same question for moment after moment and watches one of the two answers: the elevation.

Most of the time the elevation is far below zero: the satellite is round the other side of the planet. Once a lap it swings back toward us, and on a few laps the Earth has turned Clevedon near enough to the ring for the bump to clear the horizon. Those are the passes.

With ezal’s default settings, a pass is listed if it climbs to at least 10°, and it is timed from when it rises through 5° to when it sinks back through 5°: the amber line.

The search does not try every second. It takes a sample every 30 seconds. Where one sample is higher than its neighbours, a peak lies between them, and the search closes in on it by throwing away a third of the gap at a time. From the peak it steps outward a minute at a time until the elevation is under 5°, then halves that last minute, again and again, until each crossing is pinned down to a quarter of a second. It does this a little at a time, at most two positions per step, so the rest of the firmware keeps running.

Run on a computer for this chapter, ezal’s own search finds three passes in these twelve hours: 18:04:46 to 18:12:41 peaking at 11.7°, 19:41:26 to 19:53:57 peaking at 51.3°, and 21:22:12 to 21:33:55 peaking at 29.1°. The chart runs the same search on this page’s simpler orbit.

Follow one of them across the sky.

This is what the board has at the end of the calculation. For any moment of a pass it can work out an azimuth and an elevation. In the highest pass of the evening, the satellite rises in the south-east, climbs to 51° in the east-north-east, and sets in the north twelve and a half minutes later.

A direction for every moment is still only a list of numbers. The motors turn slowly, they cannot turn for ever, and they must not be started by accident.
