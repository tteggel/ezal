3. Earn permission to ask for movement

You press and hold the clockwise button on the dashboard. What does the board need to know before it takes that seriously?

The board cannot see your finger. All it ever receives is messages over Wi-Fi, and a message is weak evidence. It may have left your browser a moment ago, or sat in a queue for three seconds. You may have let go since. Your laptop may have gone to sleep with the button down. So ezal never treats a message as an order to switch a pin. It treats it as a request: something with an owner and an expiry, which dies by itself unless you keep it alive.

This chapter and the next follow one press of that button until pin GP10 goes high, stopping at each place where it can die on the way. This chapter gets the press as far as a note, waiting for the one task that is allowed to touch the pins.

How old is this message?

Twice a second the board sends every open dashboard a status report: where the dish points, who is in control, which pins are on. Tucked into each report is a token, a number the board makes up and will recognise for exactly one second. A press has to quote a token back. The board never asks the browser what time it is. A quoted token tells it that this browser has seen a report less than a second old, and that is all it wants to know.

Here is one report and one press. Make the press arrive later.

Two things came out of that. A press that quotes an expired token is refused outright. And an accepted press does not get to move the dish for as long as it likes. It gets a movement lease of 750 ms, cut short if its token expires sooner. When the lease ends the board stops asking for movement, whether or not anyone tells it to. A press that arrives 800 ms after its report is accepted, and has 200 ms to live.

The bracket along the bottom of the figure looks ahead: the board will not start a motor for a request with less than 500 ms left. Chapter 4 explains why.

Keeping it alive

Three quarters of a second is not much of a move. So for as long as the button is down, the browser sends a hold every 150 ms. Each hold quotes the newest token the browser has, and each renews the lease by the same rule: 750 ms from the moment it arrives, never past its token’s expiry. When you let go the browser sends stop, and the request ends at once.

And if the browser cannot say stop, because the lid is shut or the Wi-Fi has gone? Then the holds simply stop arriving and the lease runs out. That case is what the whole arrangement is for. Stall the Wi-Fi and watch.

A stall of half a second does no harm. The holds bunch up, land late, and find the lease still running. Past about 700 ms the lease runs out first, and then the holds that finally arrive are refused, every one.

Why not accept them and carry on? Because a hold is no evidence that you want the dish moving now. It comes from a timer in the browser, and all it says is “the press you already accepted is still down”. Once that press’s lease has run out there is nothing left for a hold to continue, and a backlog of timer ticks draining out of a queue says nothing about what you want at this moment. So the board refuses the first of them, forgets every token it has given that browser so that nothing else in the queue can get through, asks for a stop, and tells the dashboard, which lets go of the button for you. To move again you must release, wait for the next report, and press: a new decision.

One more guard rides along. Every press and hold carries a counter: 1, 2, 3 and so on. The board refuses any message whose number is not higher than the last one it accepted, so no message can be used twice, or out of order.

The numbers in those two figures are constants in the code, and the three lines under them say how they must relate.

And this is the whole of the board’s judgment on a press or a hold. Both figures have been running it.

Two things checked before any of that

Are you the one in control? Two people can have the dashboard open. Only one browser at a time is the operator; the others can watch. The first to connect gets control, and anyone may take it once it is free. The board keeps asking the operator to prove it is still there: with each status report it sends a ping down the connection (a WebSocket, which has ping and echo built in), and a prompt echo renews control for another 5 seconds. Close the tab and control is free at once. Vanish without closing it and control is free 5 seconds after the last echo. A press from anyone else changes nothing.

This stops two dashboards fighting over one dish. It does not keep strangers out: anyone who can reach the board on your network can open the dashboard and take control when it is free.

Is the board itself still on the network? A separate task looks at the Wi-Fi link ten times a second. Once its last good look is 300 ms old, nobody is operator any more and nothing may move. When the link comes back, every token issued before the gap is void, so nothing sent before or during an outage can start a move after it.

In the code: the 5-second operator lease, pings and their echoes, control claimed on connecting, and the 300 ms network check.

A note for the direction task

The press has been accepted. It is still nowhere near a pin. Chapter 1 started several tasks that take turns on the processor, and the one talking to your browser is not the one that owns the pins. It has no way to reach them. Only the direction task writes GP10, GP11, GP20 and GP21. Everyone else, the browser’s task now and the satellite tracker later, leaves it a note saying move or stop, in a mailbox with room for exactly one.

Three rules, and you can produce each of them above. A newer move replaces an older one, because only the latest wish matters. A stop is never replaced: once someone has asked for a stop, no later move can hide it before it is read. And waiting does not extend a note. A move that has expired by the time it is read is read as a stop.

Here is the browser’s task turning an accepted press into a note, and the mailbox the note goes into.

So the press has become a note: move clockwise, good until such-and-such a millisecond. Nothing has moved yet. Next we follow the note into the only task that can do something about it.

4. Cross the output boundary

A note is waiting: move clockwise, good for the next 750 ms. Will the pin now do what the note says?

Often it will not, or not yet. The direction task treats the note as a wish. What it does to the pin also depends on things the note knows nothing about: when the motor last ran, how long it has run today, where the dish is, whether the board is still on the network. So the firmware keeps two separate facts, what is requested and what is applied, and the dashboard always shows you the second.

The quickest way to see the difference is to try to make the pins misbehave. Hold a button. Let go, and press again straight away. Then try the other direction.

You will have found two things. Letting go is instant: the pin drops as soon as the direction task reads your stop. Starting is not. After a pin drops, that axis is shut for two seconds, the off-time, and it is shut in both directions. A press during the off-time is not refused. It waits, and the pin rises the moment the off-time ends, provided you are still holding.

The off-time is there for the machinery. A dish that has just lost power coasts on for a moment, and the code allows half a second for that. A position reading can be up to half a second old (chapter 2). Two seconds leaves room for both before the motor is driven again, perhaps the other way, and the firmware refuses to switch anything on if the wait before a reversal is ever set shorter than the two added together.

A matching rule sits at the other end. Once a pin goes high it is meant to stay high for at least 500 ms, the minimum on-time: a shorter click of the relays inside the G-5500 (the switches that feed its motors) moves nothing useful. This rule never holds a pin up against you: your release, a stop, or any failed check still cuts the pin at once, however short that makes the pulse. What it decides is whether a start is worth making. A pin goes high only if the request has at least 500 ms of lease left.

Those cases are hard to hit by hand, so here are four with the timing set up exactly. Step through each.

The rule behind all four is twenty lines long.

A tank of run-time

The rotator’s motors are not built to run all day. Yaesu’s manual allows five minutes of running and then asks for fifteen of rest; the manual for the DC model allows three. ezal has not been told which it is driving, so it assumes the stricter: each motor gets 180 seconds of run-time, and only fifteen unbroken minutes of rest give it back.

Three things to notice. The tank does not trickle back: a minute of rest returns nothing, fifteen return everything. Any running, however short, sets the rest clock to zero. And a restart empties the tank, because the board keeps no record across a restart and so assumes the motor has just been run hard. Skip startup rest, on the real dashboard, is you vouching that it has not. The board takes your word once per restart, only from the operator, only while nothing is moving. It never skips a rest that it has counted itself.

This is bookkeeping, not a thermometer. It counts the time ezal’s own pin was high, so it knows nothing about anyone working the switches on the front of the G-5500.

One look, in a fixed order

Now we can read the function that decides. Each time the direction task wakes, it hands Actuator::step the note if there is one, the latest position reading, the state of the network and the time. It gets back which pins may be high. The order of the lines is the order of the checks.

Writing the pins

step only returns an answer. The loop at the heart of the direction task turns it into voltages.

Then the task sleeps until something changes, or a deadline that step reported comes due, or 100 ms pass (10 ms while tracking), whichever is first. If this loop ever stalls, no stop written in software can run. The watchdog’s reset is what is left, with the caution from chapter 1 that a pin can keep its level through that reset until startup takes it back.

The last few centimetres

And so, at last, GP10 goes high. Here is everything connected to it. Switch it yourself.

A high pin is 3.3 volts, and here it does two small jobs. It lights an LED, so that you can see what the firmware is asking for. And it pushes about a milliamp through a 2.2 kΩ resistor into the base of a transistor, which is enough to switch the transistor fully on. The transistor is the whole trick. Switched on, it joins pin 2 of the G-5500’s control socket to pin 8, its ground. To the controller that is the same as someone pressing the clockwise switch on its front panel, so it does what it always does and feeds the motor from its own supply. No motor current comes anywhere near the Pico.

When the pin goes low, the 10 kΩ resistor pulls the transistor’s base to ground and the transistor lets go. The motor loses its power and the dish coasts to a stop.

One caution covers this whole chapter. None of it has yet been run on the real board and rotator. The timings are the code’s. How quickly the controller really lets go, how far the dish coasts, and whether that milliamp is enough for this particular G-5500 are still to be measured, and chapter 7 keeps the list.

A held button has become a closed switch, and you have seen each way it can fail to. Next we take the person away. Chapter 5 works out where a satellite is, and chapter 6 lets the board follow it, leaving its own notes in this same mailbox to pass through these same checks.
