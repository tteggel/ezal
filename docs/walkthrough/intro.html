An illustrated guide to ezal · no embedded experience needed · seven chapters

From power-on to pointing.

ezal is the program inside a small box that turns a dish antenna toward passing weather satellites. This page follows one request to move, from the moment the board gets power to the moment a motor turns. Then it hands the buttons to a satellite.

Here is the whole machine. Hold a button and watch what it sets off.

The antenna sits on a rotator, a Yaesu G-5500, with two motors. One turns it round like a compass needle; that angle is the azimuth. The other tilts it up from the horizon; that angle is the elevation. The motors take their power from the rotator’s own control box. ezal runs on a Raspberry Pi Pico 2 W wired to that box, and all it can do is close four switches, one per direction, as a finger would press the box’s four buttons. A motor is either on or off. There is no speed control.

Coming back the other way are two voltages that rise and fall as the antenna moves. They tell ezal where the dish is pointing, which is not always where it was told to point.

Four switches out, two voltages back: that loop is the entire machine. Everything else in ezal decides when closing a switch is allowed. The toy above asks none of those questions. It never checks that the position reading is recent, that your finger is still on the button, or that the motor has had a rest. Chapters 1 to 4 add those questions one at a time. Chapters 5 and 6 let a satellite do the asking, and chapter 7 looks at what has and has not been proven.

Every figure runs inside this page and none can reach a board. The explanation was written against main at 79046c1, 5 November 2026. The code excerpts are copied from the repository each time the page is built, so they are the current source.
